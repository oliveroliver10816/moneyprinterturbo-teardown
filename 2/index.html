Ten stories about the things you are standing in.

Method

What actually makes a thing get shared

Berger & Milkman (2012) found high-arousal emotion drives sharing — awe, anger and anxiety all increase it, while sadness decreases it. So every script is built on one specific high-arousal emotion, chosen deliberately, and no two of these five use the same one.

Information-gap curiosity

Loewenstein: curiosity is an unpleasant state that fires when you become aware of a *specific* gap in what you know. Vague mystery doesn't itch. A missing number does.

Applied: Name the gap precisely and early — 'six thousand planets and not one moon', 'wrong by nearly two feet'.

Proportionality bias

People expect big effects to have big causes. When the official explanation is small relative to the event, it feels like a cover even when it's true. This is the actual engine of conspiracy belief.

Applied: Point at a real size mismatch. Never manufacture one — the mismatch has to survive a fact-check or the whole thing collapses.

Agency detection

Humans over-attribute events to an intending actor. 'Someone chose this' sticks harder than 'this happened'.

Applied: Give the sentence a subject wherever it's honest to. 'We built the categories.' 'We're the ones running it.'

Zeigarnik / open loop

Unfinished things are remembered better and returned to. A resolved video is a finished thought and gets closed.

Applied: Never answer the question. The last line reopens it. The comments become the resolution, and that's the engagement.

Social currency

Sharing signals status — it says you know something the reader doesn't. The payoff has to be repeatable in one sentence or it doesn't get passed on.

Applied: Every script ends on a line you could say out loud to someone at work tomorrow.

Guardrails: No named living person is the subject of any theory here, nothing turns on a fresh tragedy, and every factual claim is checkable against the source listed on each card. The theory beat is always marked as a reading, not a finding.

Teardown

Every story gets pulled apart before a word is written

Seven passes over the same facts, each asking a different question. The point is that one teardown yields several videos, not one — and that pass five is the whole product.

PassThe questionWhat it yields
1. TimelineWhat happened, in what order, on what date?The spine. Gaps in the timeline are the best angles you will find.
2. EntitiesWho and what is named? Who is conspicuously *not* named?Absences are angles. A story with no named decision-maker is a story with a hole.
3. MoneyWho paid, who gained, how much, and when relative to the event?Numbers are the most shareable unit of a conspiracy. Timing beats amount.
4. The official accountWhat is the stated explanation, in its own words?You must be able to state it fairly. If you strawman it, the video dies on contact with a commenter.
5. What it does not explainWhich specific fact does the official account leave standing?**This is the video.** Not "they're lying" — "here is the thing their answer does not cover."
6. BeneficiaryIf this were deliberate, who is better off?Turns a passive event into an agent-driven one, which is what the brain latches onto.
7. The open loopWhat single question can honestly be left unanswered?The closing line. Never resolve it.

The rule that keeps it publishable: pass five has to be a real gap. If the official account covers everything, there is no video and we pick another story. And we interrogate decisions, timing, money and silence — never allege a specific crime by a named living person, because calling it a theory does not change how that reads.

36AWE

Two dozen mines detonated in thirty seconds. The cause was the sun.

Declassified US Navy records, published in Space Weather (2018)

The gap it opens

Two numbers are in the record and nobody has reconciled them: the aircrew logged two dozen, a Navy mineman wrote over four thousand.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

In nineteen seventy-two a field of American sea mines went off on its own, and the Navy classified why for forty-six years.

A US aircraft was passing a minefield off Vietnam when the water started going up.

Two dozen explosions in thirty seconds. No ship, no aircraft, no diver anywhere near them.

These were magnetic mines, built to sense a steel hull passing overhead and fire.

So the Navy investigated who had triggered its weapons, and the answer was nobody.

Ninety-three million miles away the sun had thrown out a storm of charged particles that arrived on the fourth of August.

It shoved the Earth's magnetic field hard enough that the mines read it as a passing ship.

Now think about what else along that coast was magnetic that day.

Because there is a number nobody has reconciled. A Navy mineman, Chief Petty Officer Michael Gonzales, wrote that the storm set off more than four thousand of them.

The aircrew saw two dozen. He wrote four thousand.

So which number goes in the report — the two dozen someone watched, or the four thousand someone counted?

The verified facts underneath it
  • 4 August 1972: US Task Force 77 aircrew flying near a minefield off Hon La observed 20 to 25 explosions in about 30 seconds
  • The mines were Destructor sea mines laid during Operation Pocket Money
  • The declassified US Navy report concluded there was 'a high degree of probability' the mines were detonated by the August 1972 solar storm
  • Published by Knipp and colleagues in the journal Space Weather, 2018
  • Chief Petty Officer Michael Gonzales, wartime memoirs (2015), quoted in Knipp et al. 2018: the storm caused 'the premature detonation of over 4,000 magnetically sensitive DSTs (Destructor mines)'
  • Tucker (2006), quoted in the same paper: TF-77 aircraft reported 'some two dozen explosions in a mine field near Hon La over a thirty-second time span'

37ANXIETY

A storm that could end the grid missed us by about a week.

NASA and Baker et al., Space Weather, December 2013

The gap it opens

The near miss was in 2012. Almost nobody heard about it, and the grid was never hardened.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

In twenty twelve the sun fired a storm big enough to take the grid down, and we were not standing where it landed.

In eighteen fifty-nine a solar storm hit a world with exactly one piece of electrical infrastructure, the telegraph.

Operators were shocked at their desks, and some machines kept sending after the batteries were disconnected.

That is the famous story, and it feels safe, because it happened to a world of wires and horses.

Then in July twenty twelve a cloud of charged particles left the sun at three thousand kilometres a second.

It crossed the exact orbit Earth travels on, about a week after Earth had gone past.

We know how big it was because it hit a spacecraft carrying instruments built to measure exactly this.

Now think about what that same week would have looked like with the planet standing in that spot.

The physicist who led the study, Daniel Baker, put the comparison in one sentence.

It was published, reported quietly, and most people have still never heard of it.

So what would you call a margin of one week — a warning, or luck?

The verified facts underneath it
  • 23 July 2012: a coronal mass ejection left the sun at up to 3,000 km/s, more than four times a typical eruption
  • It crossed Earth's orbit but Earth was not at that point; it struck the STEREO-A spacecraft instead
  • Had it hit, modelled Dst of about -1200, comparable to or larger than the 1859 Carrington Event
  • Study led by Daniel Baker, University of Colorado, published in Space Weather, December 2013
  • 1859 Carrington Event: telegraph systems failed and aurora were seen far outside polar latitudes
  • Daniel Baker (University of Colorado), quoted by NASA on 22 July 2014: 'In my view the July 2012 storm was in all respects at least as strong as the 1859 Carrington event. The only difference is, it missed.'
  • NASA, same article: 'If the eruption had occurred only one week earlier, Earth would have been in the line of fire.'

38ANGER

For half a century it was forbidden to say the word tornado.

NOAA Storm Prediction Center, history of tornado forecasting

The gap it opens

The word was banned to prevent panic. The storms arrived anyway, unannounced.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

For about half a century American weather forecasters were not allowed to print the word tornado.

Not discouraged. Banned, in writing, from the official forecast.

The reasoning was written down plainly, that the word provoked undue fear in the public.

The sensible version is that they were protecting people from panic, and you can almost hear the logic.

Except the storms did not stop arriving. They simply arrived without being named.

Finley had already been forecasting them in the eighteen eighties with two thousand volunteer observers, and his work went out of favour anyway.

The Weather Bureau regulation that carried the ban is one line long.

Now think about how many warnings that one line was worth over the next thirty-three years.

It was lifted in nineteen thirty-eight, and even then forecasters stayed reluctant to say it.

What broke it was a tornado hitting an Oklahoma Air Force base in nineteen forty-eight, and two officers who saw the same conditions five days later and said so in advance.

So which harm was greater — the panic they were certain of, or the storms nobody was allowed to name?

The verified facts underneath it
  • In the late 1880s the Signal Corps, and later the Weather Bureau, judged that the word 'tornado' provoked undue fear amongst the public
  • Weather Bureau policy continued to prohibit use of the word 'tornado' in forecasts
  • The restriction was lifted in 1938, though forecasters remained reluctant to use it
  • First successful tornado forecast: 25 March 1948, by Fawbush and Miller at Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma
  • A tornado had struck the same base five days earlier, on 20 March 1948
  • Weather Bureau Station Regulations (1905), verbatim: 'Forecasts of tornadoes are prohibited.'
  • Report of the Chief Signal Officer (1887), verbatim: 'it is believed that the harm done by such a prediction would eventually be greater than that which results from the tornado itself'
  • 1905 to 1938 is thirty-three years

39AWE

A sound heard three thousand miles away, and it kept coming back.

The 1883 eruption of Krakatoa and the barograph record

The gap it opens

The pressure wave went round the planet and registered again, and again, for days.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

In eighteen eighty-three a sound was heard three thousand miles from where it was made.

On Rodrigues, an island out near Mauritius, the log for that night records the distant roar of heavy guns.

There were no guns. What they heard had happened on the far side of the Indian Ocean.

At the source the pressure is estimated near a hundred and seventy-two decibels, past the point where sound stops being a sound and becomes a physical blow.

That part is astonishing, but it is not the strange part.

Because the noise faded, and the pressure wave did not.

Barometers in London, Paris, Washington, Tokyo and New York all twitched as it passed over them.

Then hours later they twitched again. The wave had gone round the planet and arrived a second time.

Now think about what was still moving over those cities while nobody in them could hear a thing.

It did it again, and instruments kept registering it for about five days.

So was that still a sound by then, or a planet that had been struck and was still ringing?

The verified facts underneath it
  • 1883 eruption of Krakatoa; the sound was heard on Rodrigues, near Mauritius, about 4,800 km (roughly 3,000 miles) away
  • British officers on Rodrigues logged it as the distant roar of heavy guns
  • Overpressure at the source estimated at about 172 decibels
  • Barometers in cities including London, Paris, Washington, Tokyo and New York recorded successive pressure spikes as the wave passed and returned
  • The wave remained measurable on sensitive instruments for around five days
  • NOTE: popular accounts differ on how many times it circled (four vs seven); the script does not state a number
  • Royal Society Krakatoa Committee report (1888), from the Rodrigues account: 'coming from the eastward, like the distant roar of heavy guns'
  • Rodrigues is about 4,800 km (roughly 3,000 miles) from Krakatoa

40UNEASE

Every banana you have eaten is a clone. The last one already died.

Fusarium wilt, Tropical Race 4, and the Cavendish monoculture

The gap it opens

We watched this exact disease erase the previous banana, then rebuilt the industry the same way.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

The banana in your kitchen is a clone, and the banana your great grandparents ate is already gone.

It was called the Gros Michel, and by most accounts it was the better fruit.

Then a soil fungus reached the plantations and killed it commercially in about twenty years.

The industry survived by switching to a backup variety that happened to resist that fungus, the Cavendish.

That is the tidy version, and it sounds like a problem that was solved.

But bananas for export are not grown from seed. They are cuttings.

About ninety-nine percent of exports are one plant, copied, across the entire planet.

Now think about what one shared immune system means for a crop grown on every continent.

Because whatever gets past one of them gets past all of them, and something has.

It is called Tropical Race Four, it goes through Cavendish, and since twenty ten it has crossed Asia into the Middle East and Africa.

The team that engineered a resistant banana wrote the position down in Nature Communications.

So do we build a new banana, or wait and find out which soil it reaches next?

The verified facts underneath it
  • The Gros Michel was driven to functional commercial extinction by Fusarium wilt, known as Panama disease
  • The industry switched to the Cavendish, which was resistant to that strain (Race 1)
  • Cavendish cultivars are the source of about 99% of banana exports and are genetic clones
  • Tropical Race 4 attacks Cavendish and many other varieties
  • Since 2010 TR4 has spread into Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, India, Pakistan, Oman, Jordan, Lebanon, Israel and Mozambique
  • There is no effective treatment; the fungus survives in soil for years as chlamydospores and resists fungicides
  • Dale et al., Nature Communications (2017), verbatim: 'There is no effective chemical control for TR4'
  • Same paper: the fungus 'can remain in the soil for >40 years'

41UNEASE

Some of the rain that falls on you may have been made by bacteria.

Ice-nucleating bacteria and the bioprecipitation cycle

The gap it opens

We still do not know what share of the world's rain is started by living things.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

Some of the rain that has fallen on you was probably started by something alive.

Rain needs a seed, because water in a cloud does not simply become a drop when it cools.

It needs a speck to freeze onto — dust, soot, salt, a grain of desert sand.

That is the textbook version, and it is correct as far as it goes.

What it leaves out is that one of the best ice-making surfaces ever measured is not mineral at all.

It is a bacterium that lives on plants, carrying a protein that freezes water where it would otherwise stay liquid.

When Christner and his colleagues went through fresh snowfall, sixty-nine to a hundred percent of the most active ice makers in it were biological.

Now think about what a bacterium gains by making the sky freeze.

It has been pulled out of rain, out of snow on a Swiss mountain, and out of clouds.

And freezing is what brings a cloud particle back down, so a bacterium that makes ice has a way home onto the plants it lives on.

So how much of the world's rain is being started by something that gets carried home when it falls?

The verified facts underneath it
  • Pseudomonas syringae carries ice-nucleation-active proteins on its outer membrane and freezes water at low supercooling
  • Ice-nucleating P. syringae has been found in rain and snow samples and isolated directly from clouds
  • Isolated from snow collected at 3,580 m at Jungfraujoch, Switzerland
  • Cells with ice nucleation activity can be preferentially precipitated out of clouds, returning the bacteria to the ground
  • The share of global precipitation that is biologically nucleated is genuinely unresolved and actively researched
  • Christner, Morris, Foreman, Cai and Sands, Science 319(5867):1214 (2008), verbatim from the abstract: 'Of the IN larger than 0.2 micrometer that were active at temperatures warmer than -7 degrees C, 69 to 100% were biological, and a substantial fraction were bacteria.'

42AWE

We pumped so much water out of the ground that the planet tilted.

Seo et al., Geophysical Research Letters, 2023

The gap it opens

Nobody set out to move the Earth's axis. It was a side effect of drinking and farming.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

Between nineteen ninety-three and twenty ten human beings moved the Earth's pole about eighty centimetres.

Not the magnetic pole. The rotational one, the actual axis this planet spins around.

And we did not do it with anything dramatic. We did it with wells.

A spinning object balances according to where its mass sits, so move enough weight and the axis moves to match.

It is why a washing machine walks across the floor when the load goes to one side.

Now think about how much water a planet has to lose from one place before the spin notices.

About two thousand one hundred and fifty gigatons, pumped out of the ground in seventeen years.

Most of it ran to the sea and raised the global sea level by a little over six millimetres.

The geophysicist who led the study, Ki-Weon Seo, ranked it against every other climate cause they measured.

Nobody planned this — it is the sum of irrigation and drinking water, well by well.

So what do you call a side effect that size — an accident, or simply what all of us add up to?

The verified facts underneath it
  • Study led by Ki-Weon Seo, Seoul National University, published in Geophysical Research Letters, 2023
  • About 2,150 gigatons of groundwater depleted between 1993 and 2010
  • Earth's rotational pole drifted about 80 cm (31.5 inches) east over that period
  • That water added roughly 6.24 mm to global sea level
  • Groundwater redistribution had the largest effect on polar drift among climate-related causes studied
  • Heaviest depletion in western North America and northwestern India
  • Ki-Weon Seo (Seoul National University), AGU press release 15 June 2023, verbatim: 'Our study shows that among climate-related causes, the redistribution of groundwater actually has the largest impact on the drift of the rotational pole.'

43UNEASE

Two in a hundred people hear a sound instruments cannot find.

The 1993 investigation into the Taos Hum

The gap it opens

The hearers could reproduce the sound. The microphones recorded nothing.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

In one town roughly two people in every hundred could hear a sound that no microphone could pick up.

They described it the same way, a low throb like an engine idling outside, worse indoors and worse at night.

The obvious answer is that people hearing what nobody else hears are describing something inside their own ears.

So a team was assembled to settle it, from the university, Sandia, Los Alamos and the Air Force laboratory.

They surveyed over fourteen hundred residents and put instruments in the town.

Now think about what it would take for dozens of strangers to invent the same frequency.

Because the hearers could reproduce it: given a tone generator they dialled in what they were hearing, and they agreed with each other.

Thirty-two to eighty hertz with a slow pulse, which is not what random inner-ear noise looks like across dozens of unconnected people.

So the team went looking for a real signal at those frequencies, and Mullins and Kelly wrote what came back.

The equipment went home, and the hum was still there.

So which is stranger — a sound with no source, or a town that agrees on it?

The verified facts underneath it
  • 1993: a congressionally requested investigation into the Taos, New Mexico hum
  • Team drawn from the University of New Mexico, Sandia National Laboratories, Phillips Air Force Laboratory and Los Alamos National Laboratory
  • Final report written by Joe Mullins (UNM) and Horace Poteet (Sandia)
  • A survey of 1,440 residents led the team to extrapolate roughly 2% of the population were hearers
  • Hearers described a frequency range of about 32 to 80 Hz with a slow pulsing quality
  • Affected residents could replicate the sound they heard; sensitive microphones and acoustic equipment detected no matching signal
  • Mullins and Kelly, 1993 Taos investigation, verbatim: 'no known acoustic signals that might account for the hum, nor are there any seismic events that might explain it'
  • James Kelly was a hearing research scientist at the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center; Joe Mullins (UNM) coordinated the team

44UNEASE

The ground lights up before some earthquakes. Nobody agrees why.

Earthquake lights and the unresolved mechanism

The gap it opens

There is security-camera video of it, and still no accepted physics.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

Before some earthquakes the sky lights up. This is on video, and there is no accepted explanation for it.

The reports are very old, describing flashes, glows along a ridge line, sometimes a blue-white sheet over the ground.

For a long time science could set that aside as frightened people at night remembering badly.

Then cameras stopped being something you had to be holding.

In Peru in two thousand seven security cameras recorded the sky flashing before a magnitude eight earthquake.

Now the easy dismissal has a problem, because a security camera is not frightened and does not remember anything.

Now think about how many of these were seen in the centuries before anyone had a camera at all.

When someone went through the catalogued reports, more than nine in ten clustered where the crust is being pulled apart.

That is a real clue about rock under stress. It is not a mechanism.

The United States Geological Survey will not even say how many of the reports are the same phenomenon.

So what do you do with something that is filmed, catalogued, and still has no physics?

The verified facts underneath it
  • Earthquake lights (EQL) are reported near the time and epicentre of some earthquakes
  • Security camera video recorded bright flashes lighting the sky at Pisco, Peru before the magnitude 8.0 earthquake in 2007
  • USGS position: 'Geophysicists differ on the extent to which they think that individual reports of unusual lighting near the time and epicenter of an earthquake actually represent EQL'
  • Published analysis found more than 90% of reports come from rift environments
  • There is no agreed mechanism
  • USGS, verbatim: 'Geophysicists differ on the extent to which they think that individual reports of unusual lighting near the time and epicenter of an earthquake actually represent EQL'

45DREAD

It snowed in June and the harvest failed across the northern world.

The 1815 eruption of Tambora and the year without a summer

The gap it opens

One mountain, on the other side of the world, took a year of food from people who never heard its name.

Why this one should travel

broad-appeal set: the sun, the air, the water, the ground and the food.

Script as recorded

In eighteen sixteen it snowed in June, the crops failed across the northern world, and almost nobody alive knew why.

In New England there was frost every month that year, and six inches of snow in early June.

Farmers replanted and failed again, and the price of grain went up and stayed up.

Across Europe it was the coldest summer in a record running back to seventeen sixty-six.

Now think about how far away a thing can happen and still empty your fields.

It was not weather. It was a mountain on the other side of the planet.

Tambora erupted in April eighteen fifteen and threw enough material into the sky to dim the sun for a year.

Global temperature dropped by well under a degree, and that was enough to take the harvest.

Beside a lake in Switzerland that summer Byron described the day the sun went out, and in the same house Frankenstein was written.

Nobody in Vermont or Bavaria could have connected the two. They had never heard the mountain's name.

So how much of a year that ruins you gets decided somewhere you have never been?

The verified facts underneath it
  • Mount Tambora erupted on 10 April 1815 in the Lesser Sunda Islands
  • Global temperatures fell by roughly 0.4 to 0.7 degrees Celsius
  • 1816 became known as the Year Without a Summer
  • European summer temperatures in 1816 were the coldest on record between 1766 and 2000
  • A snowstorm hit northern New York and New England on 6-7 June 1816, with several localities recording 6 inches
  • Crop failures and food shortages across the Northern Hemisphere; cited as a cause of farmer migration from the Northeast to the Midwest in the 1820s
  • Byron and the Shelleys were at Lake Geneva in 1816; the weather kept them indoors and Frankenstein came out of it
  • Byron on writing 'Darkness' (July 1816, Geneva), verbatim: he wrote it 'at Geneva, when there was a celebrated dark day, on which the fowls went to roost at noon, and the candles were lighted as at midnight'
