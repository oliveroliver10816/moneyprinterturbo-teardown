Going worldwide

You asked for a conspiracy platform that goes worldwide crazy famous — the way DIY and quirky one-minute videos did. Here is the mechanism those formats actually used, the free lever we already own, the one thing blocking three big languages, and the uncomfortable thing the biggest faceless conspiracy accounts are really doing to get their numbers.

The mechanism

The formats that went global were the ones you didn't have to translate

5-Minute Crafts is one of the largest channels on YouTube, and the reason is not the crafts. It is that the videos are built to be understood with the sound off, in any country — bright colours, top-down shots of hands, fast cuts, almost no spoken language. Nothing needs localising, so one upload serves every market at once.

Our videos are the structural opposite. One hundred percent of the story is carried by English narration. Strip the audio and a viewer gets stock footage and a caption they may not read. That single fact is what stands between this channel and a global audience — not the topics, not the edit, not the production quality.

There are two honest ways out, and only one of them is cheap.

Become visual-native — rebuild so the pictures carry the story

This is what 5-Minute Crafts actually did. For us it would mean abandoning the narration-led format entirely and finding a way to tell a documents-and-dates story in pictures. That is a different channel, and archival conspiracy material does not lend itself to it.

Stay narration-led, but make the narration nearly free to replace

Keep the format. Swap the voice and the captions per language, reuse everything else. This is the one that fits what we have already built — and it turns out we can do it today, at no cost.

The lever we already own

Everything below the voiceover is already language-agnostic

The composer takes an audio file and a subtitle file and builds the entire video from them — segment timing, still selection, dolly moves, sound design, the caption track, the evidence stamps. It never reads English. Change the voiceover and the subtitles, and the same 36 stories become 36 videos in another language, with no new footage, no new research and no new fact-checking.

The voice engine we already use — Edge TTS — is free, needs no API key, and is already installed on this box. I queried it directly rather than trusting documentation:

Proven, not assumed

I generated a Spanish voiceover of the Satoshi script through the real pipeline and checked what came out the other end. The accented characters survive and the word-level timings are correct, which is what the caption track is built from:

1 00:00:00,100 --> 00:00:03,485 Existe una cartera que contiene setenta mil millones de dólares 2 00:00:03,801 --> 00:00:07,030 y durante dieciséis años casi nada ha salido de ella 3 00:00:07,928 --> 00:00:09,646 Todo el mundo conoce la historia

So the localisation play is real and it is close. The remaining work per language is translating 36 short scripts — and that is writing, not engineering.

The blocker

The caption font, not the voice, is what actually limits which languages we can ship

Edge TTS will happily speak Hindi, Arabic and Bengali. Our captions will not render them. The composer burns subtitles using a single font file, and a font can only draw the characters it contains. I tested its character coverage directly against the languages worth targeting:

The three that fail need a Noto font dropped in first — a small job, but a silent disaster if nobody checks and we render a hundred Hindi videos full of blank rectangles.

What the rivals are actually doing

The faceless conspiracy accounts with the huge numbers are winning by making it up

You asked at the start of the week whether I had tracked the channels in this niche. I had, but the work was never written down anywhere and it was lost. Here it is properly, and the finding is not what I expected.

Media Matters documented the biggest faceless conspiracy operations on TikTok in a piece dated 8 February 2024. The numbers are large and the mechanism is unambiguous:

The uncomfortable part

Every one of those stories is invented. Wendigos, krakens, a surviving saber-toothed tiger, captured vampires. This is not a niche where careful sourcing is being rewarded — it is a niche where the biggest numbers belong to people making things up, and the reason the story travels is that no real event constrains how good the hook can be.

That is the growth curve you would be copying, and it is the exact thing we ruled out. I am not going to quietly drift toward it, and I would not recommend chasing those numbers.

But one thing they prove is worth taking

The same operator runs an English account and a Spanish account. 342 million views on one, 329 million on the other — Spanish is not a junior market here, it is nearly half the total. They have already proved that this exact content localises, and that the second language roughly doubles the audience.

We can copy that mechanism without copying the fabrication. It is the single strongest argument for the multi-language plan above.

Where the real money in the niche sits

The largest genuine channel in this space, The Why Files, is in the millions of subscribers — but it is long-form, human-hosted and funny, with a few hundred videos built over about five years. It is a different business from ours and not a template we can copy with a synthesised voice.

So the honest map is this: nobody has won this niche with faceless short-form built on true stories. That is simultaneously the opportunity and the warning. The opportunity is that the position is empty. The warning is that it may be empty because the fabricated version simply out-performs the true one on a scroll-based feed.

The position

Our only defensible edge is that ours survive being checked

If we cannot out-hook the invented stories, we should compete where they are weak. A wendigo video dies the moment a viewer searches the claim. Ours are built the other way round: a real document, a real date, a real number, and the official explanation given fairly before the theory is offered as a theory.

That suggests a simple, repeatable identity — the channel that shows the receipt. The evidence stamp already burned into every video (the year, the figure, pulled out of the caption and set in the upper third) is exactly that signal, and it is the thing to lean into rather than dilute. It is also, usefully, the one element that reads instantly with the sound off and needs no translation at all.

A number I am deliberately not quoting

The claim that "85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" is everywhere, and it is the kind of thing I would normally use to argue for caption-first design. I traced it: it comes from Digiday, 17 May 2016, and it is publisher self-reporting — LittleThings and Mic each estimated 85% for their own pages, while PopSugar in the same piece reported a range of 50 to 80%. Facebook never published the figure.

So it is a ten-year-old estimate that hardened into a fact through repetition — the same shape as the TikTok RPM number we already struck. The design conclusion still holds, because feeds genuinely do autoplay muted, but the number itself should not be used to justify anything.
